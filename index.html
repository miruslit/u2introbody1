The introduction

An introduction has three jobs

Order matters: context first, thesis last. The reader needs the background before your judgment makes sense.

Model introduction · Jordan Alvarez on “Lock the Phones Away”

Find the three jobs

In the summer of 2026, Illinois passed a law that will keep students’ phones put away for the entire school day, starting in the 2027–28 school year. Many students will see this law as a punishment, so anyone who supports it has to win over a doubtful audience. Marcus Hale, a high school English teacher, takes on that job in his essay “Lock the Phones Away.” He writes for parents, teachers, and community members who will help their districts decide how strict the new rule should be. In “Lock the Phones Away,” Marcus Hale uses a personal story, national statistics, and an honest answer to critics to persuade readers that districts should adopt the strictest version of the law, and his argument is mostly effective, even though a few of his comparisons go too far.

Model body paragraph 1 · Jordan Alvarez

See the six steps

Hale opens with pathos by telling the story of Maya, a junior whose phone buzzes during an important test. Even though she never touches the phone, she admits, “I read the same question four times” (Hale 1). This detail creates sympathy, because most students have felt a buzz pull their focus away. Hale returns to Maya in his conclusion, writing that she “passed her test, barely” (Hale 2). This story frames the whole essay, so readers begin and end with a real student instead of a policy. Hale also uses loaded language when he warns that attention is being “stolen one buzz at a time” (Hale 2). The word “stolen” makes distraction sound like a crime, which raises the emotional stakes. This use of pathos is effective because it makes a statewide law feel personal, and it works even better because Hale backs it up with facts.

Two quotes is the minimum. Alvarez adds a third because he has more to explain.

Quotes and citations

Fix the quote

Cite with the author’s last name and page. Norton essays: the book page (Kristof 178). Articles: front = 1, back = 2 (Okafor 2).

Steps 3 and 5

Explain or retell?

Retelling says what happens. Explaining says how the words work on the reader.

Maya’s phone buzzed during her test, and she read the question four times.
The word “stolen” makes distraction sound like a crime.
Hale says that 72 percent of high school teachers think phones are a problem.
By comparing grade levels, Hale shows that the problem is worst in high school.
